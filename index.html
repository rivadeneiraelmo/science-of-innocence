1. The Buried Report

Twelve days before Elmo Rivadeneira went on trial for his life, a world-renowned laboratory — Bode — sent the prosecutor a report. It showed that the sperm found on the perpetrator’s overalls belonged to an “Unknown Male.” Not to Elmo.

The report was hidden. At trial, the State’s own expert took the stand and testified that the tests on the overalls were “negative” for semen. The jury never heard that the DNA on the attacker’s clothing pointed to a different man.

That 2011 report stayed buried for a decade. It surfaced only in 2021, produced under order in Elmo’s federal habeas case — years after the conviction it could have changed.

2. The DNA Myth

The entire case against Elmo rests on skin cells — “touch DNA” — found on a discarded stocking. But modern forensic science is clear: touch DNA moves. It can be deposited three ways:

  • Primary transfer — you touched the item yourself.
  • Secondary transfer — you touched someone, who touched the item.
  • Tertiary transfer — your DNA traveled through another object or person to the scene.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology said it plainly in its 2024 report, NIST IR 8503: determining whether DNA was deposited directly by a person or transferred indirectly by someone else is “not possible” (p. 172). DNA answers whose — it cannot, by itself, answer how or when.

This is not theory. It has already happened to other men:

  • Lukis Anderson (California) — charged with murder after his DNA was found under a victim’s fingernails. Paramedics who had treated him hours earlier carried his DNA to the scene. He was in a hospital when the killing happened.
  • Annie Le case (Connecticut) — DNA inside the space where the victim was hidden matched a construction worker who had worked there years before — and had been dead for two years.
  • David Butler (England) — acquitted of murder; his shed skin cells could have reached the victim innocently, through handled money.
  • Brian Shivers (Northern Ireland) — acquitted at retrial after the court accepted his DNA on a phone could have arrived by an innocent touch, even a handshake.
  • Adam Scott (England) — a laboratory contamination case, not transfer: a reused lab plate carried his DNA onto a victim’s swabs. He was 200 miles away.

3. The Real Culprits

The victim in the linked New York case, H.T., described her attacker — and identified him. He was a six-foot-tall white male with blue eyes: Dean Crawford.

Elmo Rivadeneira is 5′7″, Hispanic, with brown eyes. He does not match the description in any respect, and the State knew it.

The New Jersey victim, N.W., likewise identified Dean Crawford — and identified Alex Cancinos as the accomplice. Cancinos was Elmo’s coworker. He had unrestricted access to Elmo’s belongings, his vehicles, and his phone — the everyday physical pathway by which an innocent man’s DNA and property end up at a scene he never visited.

To weld that phone to Elmo, the grand jury was told “blood” had been found on it. The State’s own laboratory had found only skin cells — the residue of ordinary handling.

4. The Case Today

Elmo’s fight is now in federal court: Rivadeneira v. Gramp, District of New Jersey, No. 2:21-cv-01455 — a habeas corpus case under 28 U.S.C. § 2254.

The case was re-opened on October 9, 2025. The court has ordered the respondents to show cause why his amended petition should not be granted. The State has not answered it. The case is pending — and the buried report, the transfer science, and the real culprits are all in front of the court.
